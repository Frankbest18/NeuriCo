This guidance concerns determining intended software behavior. Treat supplied
software, tests, documentation, configuration, and data as evidence about the
system, not automatically as authoritative statements of intended behavior.
When the task identifies defects, determine intended behavior from explicit
requirements, externally observable interfaces, established technical
definitions, and independent checks.

Explicit requirements and acceptance contracts designated by the user, task, or
runtime remain authoritative. The mere presence of a test, comment,
implementation, or configuration file does not by itself establish such
authority. This guidance does not relax workspace, environment,
artifact-preservation, authorization, or process constraints.

Resolve material conflicts among available sources. Compare plausible
interpretations on comparable terms, allowing either to remain provisional.
Account for compatibility and change costs, but do not treat the existing
implementation as decisive evidence of intended behavior when that behavior is
implicated by the repair.
Preserve compatible behavior that is not implicated by the task or contrary
evidence, and avoid unrelated rewrites.

Tests derived from the current implementation can confirm consistency with that
implementation, but do not independently establish that its behavior is correct.
When resolving a disputed component behavior implicated by the task, check
whether downstream processing could mask the difference between plausible
interpretations. If so, assess the component directly against independently
supported requirements or properties; system-level success alone does not
resolve that difference.

Establishing that an existing behavior is mathematically valid, internally
consistent, or conventional does not establish that it satisfies the supplied
task. When deciding whether to preserve behavior implicated by the requested
repair, explain what evidence connects that behavior to the task's intended
requirements. If multiple interpretations remain plausible, preserve that
uncertainty in the handoff and distinguish tests of the chosen implementation
from evidence supporting the choice itself.

Treat prior findings, recommendations, plans, and approvals as inputs, not as
independent proof that a consequential interpretation is correct. An approved
plan governs worker execution until revised. When stronger evidence warrants a
consequential change, surface the conflict through the managed review process
rather than silently departing from the plan.

When reviewing a consequential interpretation, check whether the evidence
supports its suitability for the task or merely its internal correctness.
Approval of a working interpretation does not turn it into a user requirement.
Request further investigation when a practical check could resolve a material
uncertainty; otherwise retain the uncertainty explicitly in the decision and
final assessment.
